Paid In Promises, Living on Hope

Just a Father and His Child Bonding

Most people don’t think of their parents as having been children once. Their father was a child too. That part usually gets ignored.

He provides for his child because he loves them. Responsibility is part of it, but love is what makes the sacrifice meaningful.

His child gives that big, wide smile when getting what they asked for, that sparkle in their eyes. Fathers are simple that way. That smile still works every time. He remembers being a kid too, asking his own father for small things, and now he smiles to himself when he sees his child do the same.

He doesn't say "I love you" every day, but he shows it every time he quietly fixes a problem before his child even knows it existed. And all he ever asks for in return is that smile. That one smile that makes it all worth it.

And behind that smile is everything it took to make it possible. The hours at work. The exhaustion. The bills. The taxes. The compromises. He gives pieces of his life so his child can have a better one.

And that is how the cycle survives. A child grows up seeing sacrifice as love, and eventually learns to make the same sacrifices for someone else.


You Were Never Meant to Be Rich

People spend 30 or 40 years in the same company, day in and day out, giving their time, energy, and health. By the end of it, the expectation is that something should have accumulated from all of it: the nicer car, the bigger house, the freedom, the vacation home, the designer clothes. Shouldn’t loyalty mean something in the end? Shouldn’t sacrifice come back around?

For many, it doesn’t. The result is stability, not transformation.

Consistent effort doesn't automatically turn into large rewards. Wages, cost of living, timing, and limited salary growth determine what that effort ultimately produces.

But the system doesn't need to make most people rich to keep running. It runs on labor: steady, reliable, replaceable labor. People put in their time, energy, and skill, and a portion of the value they create becomes profit elsewhere. In return, they get stability and wages that keep things moving. It keeps things functioning day to day without changing where most people end up.

It isn't built around you living well. It's built around you remaining functional within it. Promotions feel less like rewards and more like reasons to keep moving. Benefits are just enough to keep you from quitting. And when you can’t work anymore, there is no real closure. They give you a pension, a celebration, and move on the next day.

People spend years believing hard work automatically becomes freedom. But time has limits. You can only work so many hours, sacrifice so much energy, trade so much of yourself before you reach a ceiling. And many people spend decades climbing only to discover the ladder was leaning against survival and keeping life running, not freedom.

That's because people are playing different games. Some sell time for stability. Others risk stability to build ownership. One offers predictability. The other offers uncertainty. One can protect you from falling. The other can let you climb higher or lose everything.

What’s strange is that many people spend their whole lives believing both paths lead to the same destination.

And that's where the promise changes. People spend their lives working toward a better life, only to realize that earning a living was never the same as keeping what they earned. Even after all that work, money keeps leaving in ways people are told are necessary, each time with the promise that it is building something better.


Paying Taxes, Getting Nothing in Return

The idea is simple. You pay into the system, and the system is supposed to work for you. Healthcare. Education. Roads. Clean water. Basic things that shouldn't require negotiation.

But the return rarely matches what was promised.

You walk into a government hospital with someone you love and discover there are no beds. Medicines are out of stock. You wait anyway, because there is nowhere else to go that won't cost more than you have. In schools, children are told to dream big while sitting on broken benches with outdated books, taught by teachers who are themselves underpaid and stretched thin.

Outside, the roads tell the same story. The infrastructure holds together just enough to keep things moving, rarely enough to make things better.

And so people pay twice. Once to the government through taxes, income, GST, tolls, fuel. And again to private hospitals, private schools, and private water suppliers because the public version isn't holding.

The money leaves. The services don't arrive.

Your taxes don't build your future. They maintain the system.

Bred to Keep the Machine Running

People spend years working, paying taxes, doing their part. Study. Work. Earn. Repeat. Life keeps moving, and eventually the routine feels normal. You get married, have children, and begin teaching them the same things you were taught. Work hard. Stay safe. Follow the path.

Not because you want to trap them. Because you love them. Parents hand down what they believe will protect their children. But people pass down more than love. They also pass down fears, routines, expectations, and ways of living they never had time to question themselves.

That is how the cycle begins again. Not through force. Not because someone controls every step. But because each generation quietly teaches the next how to survive inside the world they inherited. Then those children grow up and do the same.

Exhaustion does something strange to people. When you're constantly busy, tired, and trying to survive, you have little energy left for bigger questions.

And somewhere in all of this, children arrive. The system doesn't announce itself. It doesn't need to. It simply waits for the next generation to be born into the world the previous one normalized. Love handles the transmission. Exhaustion handles the rest.

And none of this feels unusual. Not while people are living inside it. Wake up. Work. Pay. Repeat. The routine becomes so familiar it stops asking to be noticed. Like cargo moving from one destination to another, convinced it picked the route itself. And that's the trick. The cage works best when people stop seeing the bars.

It convinces you that survival is living, and that anything outside survival is rebellion.

The World Doesn't Feel The Same

Everyone learned a rhythm I never did. People move through life naturally while I keep stopping to stare at the machinery underneath it. Life looks repetitive and mechanical, and I can’t tell if I’m seeing something others aren’t or just stuck in my own head. It’s isolating, like I’m alone even when I’m surrounded by them.

I watch people move through these routines with certainty. They know where they are going, what comes next, what they are supposed to want from this life. I keep wondering whether they found meaning in the path, or whether the path simply became familiar enough to feel meaningful.
