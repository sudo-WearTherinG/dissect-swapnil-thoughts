It's About Inheritance

Human life reduced to routine, work, sleep

These people keep asking me, “Why don't you have a job?” And I usually just smile. Not because I don't have an answer. Because the answer is difficult to explain.

I wonder why people live like this. I wonder if I would too.

Let me break it down.

You wake up at 6 or 7 a.m. Take a quick bath, eat something, and then it begins.

Run to catch a rickshaw. Fight to get into the train. Stand among tired faces heading toward the same routine. Reach the office.

Work long enough, and the days blend together. Same typing. Same files. Same routines repeated often enough that you stop noticing them.

Your time, your skills, your peace of mind, all handed over to the company, which barely notices your presence.

After work, the routine returns. Train. Rickshaw. Traffic. Noise. Reach home at 8 or 9 p.m. Eat something. Watch TV for a while. Sleep.

And the next morning, it begins again.

You started treating exhaustion like proof that you were doing life correctly.

It slowly stops feeling like life and becomes repetition.

And what do you have to show after 20 or 30 years of this?

A second-hand car? A 1BHK flat with EMI running till you're 60? A fridge, a cooler, a TV bought on discount.

You don't own your time. Your body is tired. Some dreams slowly move further out of reach. Eventually, you become someone who is only “doing his duty.”

That's the reward? For decades of sacrifice?

A Mother’s Daily Work

And this loop isn't just for men. Women inherit a different routine.

Wake up before everyone, cook for husband and kids, pack tiffin. Clean. Drop kids. Do housework. Serve lunch. Rest for an hour. Then tea time, evening snacks, kids' homework, groceries. Make dinner. Serve husband. Clean again. Sleep.

No weekends. No salary. No “thank you.” Just expectations.

She does it all for her husband, her kids, and everyone around her. She gives her life to keeping their lives running.

Expectations become so normal that her effort disappears into routine. Meals appear. Clothes get folded. The house keeps moving. Because everything continues working, people slowly stop noticing the work itself.

Be a “good wife,” a “good mother.” Always smiling, never tired. And repeat.

They say “family is everything,” but over time her life quietly becomes organized around everyone else's needs.

In all of this, a simple question rarely gets asked:

“Is she even living?”

She spends years giving her life to others, slowly growing tired down to her bones.

Individuals in Replaceable Systems

You're taught to survive, not to question.

Some questions never make it into classrooms.

Education is presented as a ladder. Study hard, climb higher, build a better life.

But someone designed the ladder.

Someone decides what gets taught and what gets ignored. Someone writes the curriculum. Someone funds the institutions. Someone decides which skills are valuable enough to reward and which ideas are left outside the classroom.

Those choices shape what you learn to value.

The knowledge you receive shapes the way you see the world. The questions you ask, the possibilities you imagine, even the limits you accept.

When enough people are taught to value the same things, think in similar ways, and pursue the same paths, the system no longer needs to force obedience. It simply reproduces itself through education.

Our schools don't teach children how to think. They teach them how to fit into society, without realizing that’s what they’re teaching.

Year after year, school felt less like a place for questions and more like a place for memorising textbook answers.

Sit straight. Don't talk. Memorize. Pass the exam. Become a good boy, good girl, and later, a good worker.

You weren’t taught to sell your time. You were taught to get a job.

Like that was noble. Like that was freedom.

You spend years helping larger systems grow, while struggling to build stability in your own life.

You keep showing up, waiting for things to finally make sense. The routine simply continues.

You’re part of systems that could continue moving without you tomorrow.

Over those years, you learn what the world expects from you. Study this. Pass that. Get a degree. Find a job. Build a career.

You aren't taught to question. You grow up learning that staying quiet and adjusting is easier.

But somewhere in all those years, where do you learn how to question the life you're being prepared for?

Only this: “Do your job, settle down, have kids, be thankful, and reproduce more workers, just like you.”

You call earning ₹30k or ₹50k per month success? For many people, it barely provides breathing room.

The moment income stops, financial stability starts collapsing. Stability depends on continuing the cycle. That's what a job slowly becomes. Not just a career. A drug. Every month brings relief. And every month requires another dose.

You see it in the evenings. The tired eyes. The silence on the ride home. People returning with just enough energy left to repeat tomorrow.

You say, “But what else can we do?”

Beliefs become so familiar they stop feeling like beliefs at all.

Ideas like these become normal: you're powerless, and stepping outside the script means failure.

Following the script doesn't create the freedom people expect. Large systems reward predictability more than independent thinking.

One leave application and they're already training someone else. Work moves on surprisingly fast. Companies move. Systems move. You realize how replaceable you are to the structure.

That desk, that system, that ID card. It's not yours. It never was.

Over time, you begin organizing your life around expectations that aren't your own.

If you work too much, they call you responsible. If you rest or think differently, they call you lazy.

So when people ask, “Why don't you have a job?” I sometimes wonder if they're asking something else.

The real question is: Why aren't you following the routine everyone else follows?

I don't think the question comes from cruelty.

Little by little, busyness looked like purpose, and repetition looked like life.

I could be wrong. Life was meant for more than waking up tired just to earn the right to repeat tomorrow.

I've spent too much time standing at the edges, watching the routine repeat itself.

From Childhood Innocence to Adult Structure

And then, somewhere along the way, you quietly hand the cycle to your children.

When your children are small, you let them be. Running around barefoot in dusty lanes, chasing pigeons, laughing over nothing. They draw on walls, break things, ask endless questions. You call it “masti,” and you smile.

But the moment their fingers can hold a pencil firmly, everything changes.

You stop calling it innocence. Now it's “focus.” You stop listening to their silly stories. Now it's “study time.” You stop celebrating their curiosity. Now it's “learn what's in the syllabus.”

You sit them down and begin handing them dreams. Not their dreams, but yours. Engineer. Doctor. IAS. Something “respectable.”

You do it out of love. But somewhere between expectations and report cards, curiosity starts sharing space with pressure.

Now, the house transforms into an extension of the school. Every table turns into a study desk, and every conversation becomes a lecture.

And when your child comes close for a hug, it comes with fear. Because the hug might turn into another lecture on “How life works” or “How hard success is.”

This is where your child learns:

Love starts feeling conditional. Approval must be earned. Rest begins feeling like guilt. And life? It's not meant to be lived. It's meant to be passed like an exam.

And so children inherit the same fears and expectations that raised everyone before them.

You did everything they told you. Listened to your father. Followed the script.

Study hard. Get a job. Get married. Have kids.

Be a responsible man. Be a good husband. Don't question, just provide.

Why the Cycle Keeps Running

And that's the strange thing about people.

A father wakes up and does it again for thirty years, not because he loves trains, offices, or exhaustion. Because he loves someone waiting at home.

A mother keeps moving through endless routines, not because housework itself is beautiful, but because love quietly disguises itself as ordinary work.

And children? Children spend years trying to become something because somewhere deep down, they want the people they love to feel proud.

Strange, how love keeps everything running.

Truly fascinating creatures.

They suffer, sacrifice, and keep going. Then somehow call it responsibility, duty, or love.

Life on Medication After Retirement

Years later, you're retired. The world says you've earned your rest.

But today, as you sit at the dining table, there's a noticeable stillness in the air. No rush. No meetings. No deadlines. Just a peaceful quiet.

You look around.

A strip of pills sits by your tea, the only new thing the morning brought.

Your son is getting ready for office. Same tired eyes, same fast breakfast, same silence.

Your daughter is juggling a laptop, her tea, and time. She's already late.

And then you glance at your wife. She's aged. The lines on her face weren't there before.

The same hands that once held yours now move through the morning chores. Like she's still on the clock.

And suddenly, it hits you.

You didn't escape the cycle. You made it more comfortable, then handed it to your kids.

You spent your life doing what your father said. And now your children are doing what you did.

Same cycle. Same inheritance. Different generation. Just faster phones and newer EMIs.

You always thought one day you'd break free. Then you hoped your children would.

But here you are. Watching your own children walk into the same routine that once exhausted you.

And for the first time in years, your heart feels heavy. Not for yourself, but for them.

You want to stop them. You want to say, “This isn't living.”

But you don't.

Because your father never said it either.

So you just sit there… and sip your tea.

And later that evening, you glance into the mirror.

For a moment, you don't recognize yourself.

The black hair is mostly gone. The eyes are dull. The skin feels unfamiliar.

You whisper to your reflection: After all that... what exactly was exchanged for all that time? What do I have? Retirement savings? Where did my youth go?
